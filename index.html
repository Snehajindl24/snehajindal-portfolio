Flask PostgreSQL Debugging · 2 min read

My PennyWise users kept losing their data — here's how I fixed it.

The Bug

PennyWise is my personal finance tracker. One Monday morning, I logged in and everything was gone. Every transaction. Every budget. My account itself — wiped.

The app worked perfectly. It just had zero memory of ever knowing me.

This wasn't a bug in my code. It was a bug in my understanding of how the cloud actually works.

The Problem

I had shipped a Flask app on Render's free tier using SQLite. SQLite stores everything in a single .db file on the server's filesystem.

Turns out, Render's free tier uses an ephemeral filesystem. Every redeploy — or every spin-down after inactivity — wipes it. My database, along with every user's data, got deleted on every restart.

I had been running a demo, not a product.

The Fix

I migrated to Supabase Postgres. Took under thirty minutes:

  1. Spin up a Supabase project. Grab the Transaction Pooler URI (port 6543 — not the direct 5432, which breaks on serverless hosts).
  2. Add DATABASE_URL as an env var on Render.
  3. My config.py already had a fallback: if the env var exists, use Postgres; else, fall back to local SQLite. Zero code changes needed.
  4. Redeploy. Tables auto-created. Done.

I made a fresh account, triggered a manual redeploy — the exact action that used to nuke everything.

Everything was still there.

The Lesson

"It works locally" is a trap. Production isn't just your code on someone else's computer — it's a different set of constraints. Read the docs before you deploy anything you'd actually miss.

Free tiers are free for a reason.

PennyWise is live at pennywise-ztq5.onrender.com.

AI LLM Finance · 2 min read

Why a CS student took four finance electives.

The Elective Nobody Picked

Third year at Thapar. Elective enrollment opens. My batch picks Cloud, AI, and Mobile Dev.

I pick Derivative Markets.

One friend asked if I was switching to an MBA. I wasn't. But everyone around me was optimizing for the same three career paths and building the same three portfolio projects. I wanted an unfair advantage.

Turns out, it was two hours of financial modeling every week.

The Insight That Became StockPulse

Halfway through the course, we studied sentiment as a market driver — the idea that stock prices reflect the stories investors tell, not just the fundamentals.

Meanwhile, in CS, we were exploring LLMs and how well they read unstructured text.

The connection was obvious: LLMs are extraordinarily good at reading sentiment from news. And sentiment moves stocks.

Existing tools for this are either enterprise-priced (Bloomberg) or oversimplified retail apps. I wanted to give a retail investor real signal quality, for free, in under two seconds.

That became StockPulse.

Where Finance Actually Mattered

Anyone can wire up an API. What the finance classes changed was prompt design.

My first prompt was naive: "Is this news positive or negative?" The model kept flagging "record profits" headlines as bullish — even when the stock was falling because guidance was weak.

Financial Statement Analysis had taught me why: markets don't react to news; they react to news relative to expectations.

I rewrote the prompt to weight surprises over confirmations, and forward guidance over trailing results. Signal quality jumped immediately.

Not because the model got smarter — because I asked a smarter question.

The Takeaway

You don't have to pick finance. But pick something outside CS.

That extra lens changes how you reason about systems, risk, and incentives.

For me, it eventually showed up in market sentiment, portfolio decisions, and the dashboards I now build.

Power BI DAX Finance · 3 min read

Building a financial statement dashboard that tells the truth behind the numbers.

The problem with raw statements

Financial statements are full of useful information — but rarely in a format someone can act on quickly.

Even when you have revenue, margins, debt and cash flow in a spreadsheet, the patterns are easy to miss. Sector averages hide company-specific problems, and a bright quarter can still sit on weak fundamentals.

I wanted a way to move from static numbers to decisions.

From ETL to decision-ready views

The first step was cleaning the data. I used Python and yfinance to pull financial data for multiple companies across IT, Banking and FMCG, then normalized it into a star schema so the model could scale cleanly.

That meant building fact tables for revenue, profitability and balance-sheet metrics, and dimension tables for companies and sectors.

Once the data was structured, Power Query and DAX became the real force multipliers — turning raw metrics into ratios, trend logic and risk labels.

What made it useful

The dashboard included 15+ custom measures — ROE, margins, debt-to-equity, FCF, YoY growth, and a composite Financial Health Score.

That last metric mattered most. It gave rough red/amber/green signals, which made comparisons across companies far easier than reading row after row of finance tables.

The report itself was designed around the decisions a stakeholder actually makes: executive summary, trend analysis, sector benchmark comparisons, and company drill-throughs.

The key insight

One of the strongest findings was that sector-level averages hide the real story.

For example, a company like Wipro could sit at a health score of 70 while the sector-leading firms TCS and Infosys were near 100. The averages suggested stability, but drill-through revealed the underlying performance gap.

That was the moment I realized dashboards are not only reporting tools — they are decision lenses.

The takeaway

Good analytics doesn't just present numbers. It helps people see what is structurally strong, what is temporarily weak, and what deserves attention next.

In finance, the difference between a spreadsheet and a story is often one well-designed dashboard.
